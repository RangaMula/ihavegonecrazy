# Legend of Iroquois Falls

- **Tradition:** Ojibwe and Algonquin peoples (Americas)
- **Source book:** *Myths and Folk-lore of the Timiskaming Algonquin and Timagami Ojibwa*
- **Author / collector / translator:** Speck, F. G. (author, d. 1950)
- **Original language of this text:** en
- **Source:** Project Gutenberg eBook #66509 — https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/66509
- **Copyright status:** Public domain worldwide: the text was published long ago and every named author/translator died in or before 1955.

---

Once long ago a war party of Iroquois came north into this country to fight the Indians at Abitibi. On the way they captured an old woman and took her with them to guide them. [ ] When they were on Abitibi river they drew near the big falls there without knowing it. The old woman was made to stay in the first canoe. When she knew they were nearing the falls she slowed up so that the flotilla of canoes became bunched. The approach to the falls is very much concealed. All of a sudden they found themselves upon the brink of the falls, and before they could help themselves they all went over. The old woman was lost with the rest in saving her people from being massacred by the Iroquois. [ ]

The Indians often use the red willow bark to mix with tobacco. It is called məskwa′­bi·‵mij “red willow tree.” The yellow spots seen on the red of the bark are where Wiske·djak, in walking over them, got them between his legs and left yellow matter in the blood from the scabs. The “rock weed,” wa′kwund (rock tripe) is often eaten in the bush when other foods fail. It is scraped off the rocks with a flat stick into a blanket, then washed and boiled and eaten. The water becomes a little slimy, but it makes a nourishing soup.  ↑

Pαgwa′k·wut pugəma′gan “arrow-head hammer,” an old style of war club with a stone set in a big wooden head attached to a handle and swung by a thong from the warrior’s elbow to leave his hand free.  ↑
